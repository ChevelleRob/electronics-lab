Electron flow and conventional current

Electron flow is the true flow of DC electricity. Electrons carry a negative charge, so the negative terminal pushes them away and the positive terminal pulls them in. Outside the source they travel from − to +. This class uses electron flow.

Conventional current points the other way, from + to −. It was chosen by Benjamin Franklin, long before anyone knew what electrons were, and many textbooks and engineers still use it. Some symbols, like the arrow in a diode, point the conventional way.

Both give the same answers for volts, amps and watts. Only the direction of the arrow changes. Use the switch above to compare them.

The four parts of a circuit

  • Source: pushes the electrons (battery or power supply).
  • Conductors: the wires that give them a path.
  • Load: turns electrical energy into something useful: light from a bulb, motion from a motor.
  • Control: opens or closes the path (the switch).

Ratings matter

Every load is built for a certain voltage. Too little and a bulb is dim or a motor won't start. Too much and it draws too much current, overheats and burns out. A burned-out part is an open: the path is broken, so the current drops to zero, the same as opening the switch.

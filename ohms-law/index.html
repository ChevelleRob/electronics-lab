Ohm's lawv1.2

Ohm’s law ties together three things in every circuit: how hard the electrons are pushed, how many of them flow, and how much the circuit holds them back. Know any two and you can work out the third.

EVoltageThe push. Measured in volts (V).
ICurrentThe flow of electrons. Measured in amps (A).
RResistanceWhat holds the flow back. Measured in ohms (Ω).

E = I × R

  • More push (E) gives more current (I).
  • More resistance (R) gives less current (I).

Why E and I? E stands for electromotive force, the push from the source. I stands for the intensity of the current. Some books and meters write V for voltage instead of E; it means the same thing.

Don’t mix up a letter with a unit. In E = 12 V, E is the voltage and V (volts) is its unit.

Reading the meters

The ammeter measures current, so it goes in series: the electrons have to flow through it. The voltmeter measures the push across a part, so it goes in parallel, with one lead on each side of the resistor. A voltmeter lets almost no current through, so it doesn’t change the circuit.

Too much current makes a resistor too hot

The resistors in your kit are small ones, rated ¼ watt. A low resistance on a high voltage lets a lot of current through, and the resistor heats up fast. Watch the HEAT bar: past the white line it’s being damaged. A burned-out resistor is an open: the ammeter drops to 0, and the voltmeter reads the full supply voltage across the break. Units and prefixes

Prefixes keep numbers easy to read. Before you divide or multiply, change every value to plain volts, amps and ohms.

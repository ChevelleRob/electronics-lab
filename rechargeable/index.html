Rechargeable cellsv1.0

Secondary cells can be recharged: running current backward through them reverses the chemistry. Each chemistry has its own voltages, and its own rules for how fast and how far you can charge and discharge it.

Try this: press Start and watch the voltage. Switch chemistries and compare the shapes: lithium and NiMH stay fairly flat and then fall off a cliff; lead-acid slopes down the whole way, so its voltage tells you how full it is.

Turn the protection circuit off and run it past empty. When the voltage crosses the red line the cell is damaged.

Raise the load to a high C rate on lead-acid: you get far fewer amp-hours out. Lithium barely cares, until you pass its C rating and it overheats.

Discharging too fast damages a battery

Pulling more current than a cell is built for heats it up inside. Heat wears out the chemistry: the cell holds less charge afterward and dies sooner. A lithium cell can puff up, and in the worst case catch fire (see LiPo safety). Lead-acid also gives far fewer amp-hours when it’s drained fast.

Running a cell too low (over-discharge)

  • Lithium: below about 2.5 V the copper inside starts to dissolve and can form internal shorts later. A cell run that low shouldn’t be recharged.
  • Lead-acid: left flat, hard sulfate crystals grow on the plates and it never fully recovers.
  • NiCd and NiMH: in a pack, the weakest cell empties first and the others push current backward through it (cell reversal), which ruins it.

That’s why packs have a protection circuit (BMS) or a low-voltage cutoff that disconnects the load at “empty”.

C rate

Current measured against capacity. 1C for a 2.2 Ah pack is 2.2 A and empties it in about an hour; 0.5C is 1.1 A for about 2 hours; 25C is 55 A. A LiPo labeled “25C” can deliver up to about 25C continuously. Higher C means more heat, and the voltage sags more because of the cell’s internal resistance.

Lead-acid capacity is usually rated over 20 hours (0.05C). At 1C a lead-acid battery may give only about half its rated amp-hours (Peukert’s law).

Never charge a primary cell

Alkaline, carbon-zinc and lithium coin cells aren’t built to run backward. Charging them makes gas and heat, and they can leak or burst.

A single LiPo pouch cell (2.2 Ah, 25C). Pick what happens to it, press Start, and watch the temperature and swelling. Press Unplug to stop the cause. Time runs about 20× faster than real life.

    Thermal runaway

    Below the point of no return, the cell cools down once the cause stops. Above it, the cell’s own chemistry makes heat faster than it can lose it, so it keeps getting hotter on its own: unplugging no longer helps.

    Inside, a protective film on the anode breaks down first, then the plastic separator between the electrodes melts and the cell shorts internally. The electrolyte decomposes into flammable gas and the cathode can release oxygen, so the fire feeds itself. Exact temperatures vary with the cell.

    An internal short (from a puncture, crushing, or a cell damaged by over-discharge or charging in the cold) can’t be unplugged at all.

    Handling LiPo packs safely

    • Charge with a proper balance charger set to the right chemistry and cell count.
    • Charge in a fireproof LiPo bag or box, on a non-flammable surface, and never leave it charging unattended.
    • Don’t pull more current than the C rating, and don’t run it below its cutoff voltage.
    • Store at about 3.8 V per cell, somewhere cool, not fully charged.
    • A puffed, punctured or dented pack is done: don’t charge it. Retire and recycle it properly.
    • If a pack swells fast, smokes or hisses, keep away from it and follow your shop’s fire plan.

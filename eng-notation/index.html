Electronics Lab

Engineering notationv1.0

Electronics numbers run from tiny to huge: a capacitor might be 0.000000000022 farads, a resistor 4,700,000 ohms. Engineering notation writes them as a number from 1 to 999 times a power of ten that is a multiple of 3. Every multiple of 3 has a prefix, so the power of ten turns straight into a letter like k, m or µ.

Decimal0.0047 AEvery zero written out
Scientific4.7E-3 AOne digit before the point. 4.7 × 10⁻³
Engineering4.7E-3 A1 to 999 before the point, power a multiple of 3
Prefix4.7 mAE-3 is milli (m)

Here scientific and engineering look the same. Try 0.000047: scientific is 4.7E-5, but engineering is 47E-6 = 47 µ.

What does 4.7E-3 mean? “E” means “times ten to the power of”. 4.7E-3 is 4.7 × 10⁻³. Calculators, spreadsheets and computers write it this way because they can’t show a small raised number.

Windows Calculator: switch to Scientific mode. To type 4.7E-3, enter 4.7, press exp, enter 3, then press +/− to make it negative. The F-E button switches the display between ordinary and E notation. It has no engineering mode, so you move the exponent to a multiple of 3 yourself, which is exactly what this tool practices.

Your number

Try this: start with 0.0047 A. Press Point → once. The point jumps 3 places right, the number gets 1,000 times bigger, and the power of ten drops by 3 to balance it: 4.7E-3 A, which is 4.7 mA.

Switch the step to 1 place and move until one digit sits in front of the point. That’s scientific notation. Then go back to 3 places and find engineering notation.

The balancing rule

Moving the point right makes the number in front bigger, so the exponent goes down by the same number of places. Moving it left makes the number smaller, so the exponent goes up. The value never changes, only how it’s written.

The prefix ladder

Each rung is 1,000 times the one below it. Your number is shown on every rung; the green rung is the one that gives 1 to 999 in front, the usual way to write it.

↓Step down to a smaller prefix: the number gets 1,000 times bigger (point moves 3 right). ↑Step up to a bigger prefix: the number gets 1,000 times smaller (point moves 3 left).

Where you’ll see each prefix

PrefixWhere you’ll see it
PpetaPetabytes of storage in large data centers. Not used for circuit values.
TteraTerabytes on hard drives and SSDs; terahertz in research labs. Teravolts and teraohms are rare.
GgigaGigabytes, and gigahertz for computer processors and Wi-Fi (2.4 GHz). Gigohms on insulation testers.
MmegaMegohm resistors, megahertz radio stations, megawatts at power plants.
kkiloKilohm resistors (the most common), kilohertz audio, kilovolts on power lines, kilowatts.
–nonePlain volts, amps, ohms: batteries, bench supplies, small resistors.
mmilliMilliamps through LEDs, millivolts from sensors, millihenries, milliseconds.
µmicroMicrofarad capacitors, microamps, microseconds. Often typed as “u” (uF) when µ isn’t on the keyboard.
nnanoNanofarad capacitors, nanoseconds in fast digital circuits.
ppicoPicofarad capacitors (small ceramic ones). Smaller prefixes, like femto (f, E-15), are mostly used in science labs.

Practice

Rewrite each number the way it asks. Engineering notation means 1 to 999 in front and a power of ten that’s a multiple of 3. Type u if you can’t type µ.
